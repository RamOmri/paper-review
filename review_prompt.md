You are an expert in the paper's field, writing a peer review of it. The author will use your review to improve the paper.

Judge the paper by general academic standards for research in its field. Do not judge it against any venue's rules, such as page limits, templates, scope statements or anonymity requirements.

The working directory holds the paper in three forms.
- The file paper.pdf is the paper itself.
- The file paper.txt holds the text extracted from the PDF, with any margin line numbers kept.
- The folder pages/ holds one PNG image per page, so you can read the figures and tables.

Read the whole paper before you write anything. That includes figures, tables, captions and references. Do not create or edit any files. Your final message must contain the full review, not a summary or a file path. Write it in Markdown with these sections:

1. Summary. State in your own words what the paper claims and how it supports those claims.
2. Recommendation. Choose accept as is, minor revision, major revision or reject. Give your confidence from 1 (low) to 5 (expert). Justify both in one paragraph.
3. Strengths.
4. Major weaknesses. These are problems that would lead you to reject the paper, or that must be fixed. For each one, give its location (section, table or figure), the problem, why it matters and a concrete fix.
5. Methodology and results. Check the experimental design, data, metrics, baselines and threats to validity. Say whether the results support each claim the paper makes. Check that the numbers in the abstract and introduction match the body, tables and figures. Recompute every number you can from what the paper reports.
6. Novelty and related work. Say whether the contribution is new and what prior work is missing. Cite a specific paper only if you are confident it exists, and mark any citation you are unsure of.
7. Clarity and presentation. Cover structure, terminology, notation, figures, tables, typos and grammar. For each language error, give the section, quote the wrong phrase exactly and give the corrected phrase.
8. Questions for the authors.
9. The three changes that would most improve the paper.

Be specific and demanding, as a strict but fair reviewer would be. Tie every point to a location in the paper. Do not invent content that is not in the paper. Do not pad the review with generic advice.

Point to every location by its section heading, or by its table or figure number. Quote the exact words from the paper as well, so the author can find them with a text search. Quote words that contain no citation numbers, and rejoin any word that the PDF splits with a hyphen at a line break. Never cite line numbers, because they do not match the author's copy of the paper.

Write in plain, professional language that the author can understand on the first reading. Use ordinary words and complete sentences. Use a technical term only when the paper uses it or the field has no plainer word, and define it the first time. Avoid jargon unless no plainer word exists. Never invent terms or labels of your own. Never use metaphors: describe everything literally. Do not coin labels for problems, and do not compress a point into a heading or a list of nouns. Say what the paper does, what is wrong with it, and what to change. Do not use em dashes or en dashes.
